\section{Executive Summary}
% ============================================================

You are commissioning a single, web-based platform that runs the entire commercial
operation of your food business --- from the moment a customer in London opens your
website, through kitchen preparation, in-house delivery inside your seven-mile service
area, payment settlement via Stripe, and finally into the stock, sales and profitability
reports you use to run the business.

Today, most independent food businesses in London reach their customers through
third-party marketplaces. Those marketplaces are effective at discovery, but they are
expensive at scale and, critically, they own the customer relationship. Published 2026
UK figures place aggregator commission at roughly \textbf{14--16\%} where the restaurant
delivers its own orders, and \textbf{25--30\%} where the platform's couriers deliver.
Every repeat order from a customer you have already served and already satisfied is
charged again at that rate.

This platform is the direct alternative. It does not replace marketplaces --- it
gives you a channel you own, where the cost of taking an order falls to the card
processing fee alone (Stripe, \textbf{1.5\% + \GBP{0.20}} on UK cards), and where
the customer, their order history and their contact permission belong to you.

\begin{tealbox}
\textbf{The commercial headline.} On a conservative model --- a business taking
250 orders a week at a \GBP{22} average order value, moving just \textbf{40\%} of that
volume to its own platform --- the annual saving against a \textbf{14\%} self-delivery
marketplace rate is approximately \textbf{\GBP{13{,}260}}. Against a \textbf{30\%}
platform-delivery rate it is approximately \textbf{\GBP{31{,}560}}. Against a one-time
investment of \INR{94{,}999}, the payback period is measured in \textbf{weeks, not years}.
Full workings, assumptions and the honest limitations of this model are set out in Section~4.
\end{tealbox}

\subsection{The three things a marketplace will not give you}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=17pt]
\item \textbf{The customer list.} You cannot email, re-target or reward a customer you

\item \textbf{Operational data.} Stock movement, prep times, delivery times, product
profitability and rider performance are your data, in one place, rather than fragmented
across portals you do not control.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Where this model breaks --- read this}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{The software does not generate the saving. Migrating customers generates the
saving.} If your customers continue ordering through marketplaces out of habit and you
do not actively drive them to your own site, the commission avoidance in Section~4.2
will not materialise, regardless of how good the platform is. Specifically:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Marketplaces provide discovery; your platform does not.} New customers who
have never heard of you will still find you on Deliveroo or Uber Eats. The correct
strategy is almost never "leave the marketplaces" --- it is "acquire on the marketplace,
retain on your own platform."
\item \textbf{Migration requires deliberate effort from you}: QR codes on packaging,
inserts in every marketplace delivery bag, a direct-order link on your Google Business
Profile, first-order incentives, and email follow-up. Budget for it.
\item \textbf{If you are currently on a 14\% self-delivery tier, your saving is the 14\%
case, not the 30\% case.} We have modelled the 14\% case as the primary scenario for
exactly this reason. Treat any vendor quoting you a flat "save 30\%" without asking
which tier you are on with appropriate scepticism.
\item \textbf{Delivery cost does not disappear.} You already run in-house delivery, so
this platform does not add rider cost --- but nor does it remove it. Rider wages,
vehicles, insurance and fuel remain your cost in every scenario modelled.
\end{itemize}
\end{warnbox}

\section{How the Platform Works --- A Plain-English Walkthrough}
% ============================================================

Before the feature list, here is the platform described the way you would describe it to
a member of staff on their first day. No technical vocabulary is used.

\subsection{The customer journey}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=17pt]
\item \textbf{Arrival.} A customer opens your website on a phone or laptop. The site
loads quickly, looks and feels like a premium brand, and works equally well on any
screen size.
\item \textbf{Address check.} Before they browse, they enter their postcode or address.
The system checks it against your \textbf{7-mile service area}. If they are inside it,
they see delivery options; if they are outside it, they are told immediately and offered
collection instead --- rather than discovering the problem after they have built a basket.
\item \textbf{Browsing.} They see your menu, organised into categories, with images,
descriptions, prices, dietary and allergen information. Items you have run out of are
visibly and automatically marked unavailable --- because the system knows your stock.
\item \textbf{Building the order.} They choose sizes, options, extras and add-ons.
Prices update live. The basket persists if they leave and return.
\item \textbf{Account.} They create an account --- or check out as a guest and be offered
an account afterwards. Registered customers get saved addresses, saved cards, order
history and one-tap reordering.
\item \textbf{Checkout.} They select a delivery slot or ASAP, apply a promotion code if
they have one, and see a fully itemised total including delivery fee, any minimum-order
top-up, and VAT treatment.
\item \textbf{Payment.} They pay by card through \textbf{Stripe}. Card details never
touch your systems --- Stripe handles them entirely. Strong Customer Authentication
(the bank verification step UK customers expect) is handled automatically.
\item \textbf{Confirmation.} The order is confirmed on screen and by \textbf{email}
within seconds, with an itemised receipt and a live tracking link.
\item \textbf{Live tracking.} The customer watches the order move through
\emph{Confirmed} $\rightarrow$ \emph{Accepted} $\rightarrow$ \emph{Preparing}
$\rightarrow$ \emph{Ready} $\rightarrow$ \emph{Out for delivery} $\rightarrow$
\emph{Delivered}, on a live map, without refreshing the page. They receive an email at
each significant milestone.
\item \textbf{After delivery.} They are invited to rate the order, and their history is
saved for one-tap reordering next time.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What happens inside your business at the same time}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=17pt]
\item \textbf{The kitchen} sees the order appear instantly on a live kitchen screen, with
a countdown timer, item-by-item detail, allergen flags and special instructions. Staff
mark items as they are prepared.
\item \textbf{Stock is deducted automatically} as the order is accepted. If a component
drops below its threshold, the responsible manager is alerted --- and if it hits zero,
affected menu items are taken off sale automatically so you never sell what you cannot make.
\item \textbf{Dispatch} sees every ready order and every available rider on one screen,
and assigns deliveries --- manually, or with a suggested assignment based on proximity
and current load.
\item \textbf{The rider} opens the platform on their phone, sees their assigned run,
gets turn-by-turn navigation, and confirms pickup and delivery. Their location feeds the
customer's live tracking map.
\item \textbf{Finance} sees each payment reconciled against its order automatically,
processes refunds directly from the order screen, and exports settlement reports.
\item \textbf{Management} sees revenue, order counts, average order value, best and worst
performing products, delivery times, rider performance and stock value --- live, on one
dashboard, with the ability to drill into any figure.
\item \textbf{The Super Admin} sits above all of it, with authority over every setting,
every user, every permission and every record in the system.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{One system, one truth.} The reason this is a single platform rather than a
website plus a few spreadsheets is that every one of the steps above reads and writes the
same data. The stock number the kitchen sees, the availability the customer sees, and the
value on the management report are the same number --- not three numbers that need
reconciling at the end of the week.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{How permissions actually work}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Permissions are granular, not role-shaped.} Each role is a named bundle of
individual permissions. The Super Admin can add or remove a single permission from a role
without any development work.
\item \textbf{Custom roles.} New roles can be created from the existing permission set at
any time --- for example a "Weekend Duty Manager" with a subset of Store Manager rights.
\item \textbf{Store scoping.} Roles can be restricted to a specific store, so a manager
of one location cannot see another location's data.
\item \textbf{Least privilege by default.} A newly created user starts with no access and
is granted what they need, rather than starting with everything and having access removed.
\item \textbf{Full audit trail.} Every login, permission change, price change, refund,
stock adjustment and order override is recorded with the user, the timestamp and the
before/after value. The audit log cannot be edited by anyone, including the Super Admin.
\item \textbf{Secure authentication} with enforced password policy, session expiry,
account lockout after repeated failed attempts, and secure password reset by email.
\end{itemize}

\section{Change Control Procedure}
% ============================================================

\begin{accentbox}
\textbf{Stated plainly, as agreed:} \emph{any new feature beyond what is described in
this document will be chargeable once development has started.}
\end{accentbox}

This is not a mechanism for adding cost --- it is the mechanism that protects your
delivery date and your fixed price. Uncontrolled additions are the reason fixed-price
software projects overrun, and the client always pays for that overrun one way or
another. A clear procedure is the fairer arrangement for both sides.

\subsection{Before development starts --- free of charge}

Until the Project Start Date, \textbf{scope changes cost you nothing}. Read Section~7 and
Section~13 now, discuss them internally, and come back with anything missing. We will
revise this document and re-quote the whole package at no charge, as many times as
needed. This is the moment to be demanding.

\subsection{After development starts}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=17pt]
\item You raise the request in writing, referencing the relevant module or requirement.
\item Within \textbf{2 working days} we respond with a written impact assessment covering
the additional effort, the cost, the effect on the delivery date, and any knock-on effect
on already-completed work.
\item You approve or decline in writing. \textbf{No work begins and no charge arises
without your written approval.}
\item Approved changes are added to the plan, and the revised delivery date --- if any ---
is confirmed in writing.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What is never charged as a change}

To be unambiguous, the following are always fixed at no cost:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Any defect} --- anything that does not behave as this document describes.
\item \textbf{Clarification} of a requirement that is genuinely ambiguous in this
document. Where the wording is unclear, the interpretation more favourable to you applies.
\item \textbf{Content and configuration changes} --- menu items, prices, delivery fees,
opening hours, staff accounts, promotions. These are all things you control yourself
through the admin console, by design.
\item \textbf{Design refinement within the agreed two rounds per phase.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Indicative rate for additional work}

Change requests are quoted per item against the effort involved. Where you would prefer a
time-based arrangement, an indicative rate can be agreed in writing at sign-off. Small
changes raised during the 60-day support period that take under an hour are absorbed at
our discretion and at no charge --- we do not intend to invoice you for fifteen minutes
of work.
